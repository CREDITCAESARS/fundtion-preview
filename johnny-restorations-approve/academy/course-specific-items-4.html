Repossessions

A repossession happens when the lender takes back a car (or other property) after missed payments. It usually reports as a negative account, and there may be a remaining balance.

HOW A REPO REPORTS
The account often shows the late payments leading up to it, the repossession itself, and any remaining balance after the vehicle was sold. It can report for up to 7 years from the date of first delinquency.

NOTICES THE LENDER OWED YOU
State laws generally require the lender to send certain notices, for example notice of the planned sale of the vehicle and an explanation of how any remaining balance was calculated. The exact rules depend on your state. Gather every letter you received, and look up your state's rules or ask legal aid.

DEFICIENCY BALANCE ACCURACY
The deficiency is what the lender says you still owe after the sale. Check it:
the sale price and the date;
repossession, storage and sale fees that were added;
credits for anything you paid.
If the amount reported doesn't match the documents, dispute the balance with the bureau and the lender, with copies attached.

WHAT ELSE TO CHECK
The same balance and dates on all three bureaus; one account, not two for the same vehicle; a collector and the original lender not both showing a balance.

REBUILDING AUTO CREDIT AFTER
Expect higher rates for a while. Build on-time payment history on other accounts, save a larger down payment, and get pre-approved before you shop (see the Credit Goals course).
